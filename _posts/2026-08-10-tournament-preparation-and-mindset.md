Two players with the same chess knowledge can get very different tournament results. The difference is usually **preparation, time management and mindset**. These skills can be trained just like tactics.

## Before the tournament

**Two to four weeks before**

- **Play longer games** at the time control of the tournament. Your thinking speed needs to match the event.
- **Review your openings.** Don't learn new ones now. Refresh the lines you already play, and fix the holes your recent games showed.
- **Solve tactics every day**, at a level where you get most puzzles right. You want sharp pattern recognition, not frustration.
- **Revise basic endgames**: king and pawn endings, the Lucena and Philidor positions, and the basic mates.

**The last few days**

- **Reduce the workload.** Light puzzles and reviewing your own notes are enough.
- **Sleep well.** Tiredness costs more rating points than any opening gap.
- **Prepare practical things**: the venue, travel, pens, scoresheets, water, a light snack, the rules about phones and electronic devices.

## During the game

**Manage your clock**

- Spend more time at **critical moments**: when the pawn structure changes, when trading pieces, and when you are choosing a plan.
- Play **quickly in simple, forced positions** to save time for later.
- Avoid falling far behind on the clock in the early middlegame. Time trouble causes more blunders than anything else.

**Use a thinking routine** on important moves:

1. What does my opponent's last move want?
2. Checks, captures and threats, for both sides.
3. Choose two or three candidate moves and compare them.
4. Blunder check, then move.

**Write your move before playing it** (if the rules allow), then take one last look at the board.

## Managing nerves and emotions

- **Nerves are normal.** Even top players feel them. A few slow breaths before the game and during long thinks help calm your body down.
- **Play the board, not the rating.** A higher-rated opponent still has to find good moves. A lower-rated opponent still deserves full respect.
- **Don't evaluate the result during the game.** Thoughts like "if I win this, I'll be in the top three" pull your attention away from the position.
- **After a mistake, reset.** Get up, drink some water and look at the position as if it were new. The game isn't over until it's over. Many games are saved by players who kept fighting after a blunder.

## Between rounds and after a loss

- **Don't analyse every game deeply during the event.** Check briefly what went wrong, then let it go and prepare for the next round.
- **A loss is information, not a verdict.** Write down the one lesson from the game. Analyse it properly after the tournament.
- **Avoid "revenge chess"** in the next round: playing too aggressively to make up for a loss usually leads to another one.
- **Eat, rest and walk.** Your brain needs energy for the next game.

## After the tournament

Analyse all your games using our [game analysis method](/blog/how-to-analyse-your-own-games/), list the recurring problems, and turn them into your training plan for the next month.

Chess tournaments reward players who stay calm, manage their time and keep learning. Prepare well, trust your training, and enjoy the fight.
