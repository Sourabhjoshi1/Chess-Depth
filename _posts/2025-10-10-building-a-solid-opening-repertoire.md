Openings are the most over-studied and least understood part of chess. Many players spend hours memorising long lines and still feel lost by move 12. A good repertoire is not about knowing the most moves. It is about **understanding the positions you reach**, so that you know what to do when your opponent leaves the book.

## First, the opening principles

Before any specific opening, every player should follow these principles until they become automatic:

1. **Fight for the centre** with pawns (e4, d4) and pieces.
2. **Develop your pieces**: knights and bishops first, usually knights before bishops.
3. **Castle early** to make your king safe and connect your rooks.
4. **Don't move the same piece twice** in the opening without a good reason.
5. **Don't bring your queen out too early**, where it can be chased with tempo.
6. **Connect your rooks** and put them on central or open files.

If you follow these principles, you will reach a playable middlegame against almost anything, even without knowing the theory.

## Choose openings that match your style

There is no "best" opening, only openings that suit *you*. Ask yourself:

- Do I enjoy **sharp, tactical positions** where both kings are under attack?
- Or do I prefer **calm, strategic positions** where I can outmanoeuvre my opponent slowly?

| Your style | As White, consider | As Black vs 1.e4 | As Black vs 1.d4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open, tactical | 1.e4 with the Italian Game | 1...e5 or the Sicilian | King's Indian Defence |
| Solid, strategic | 1.d4 with the Queen's Gambit, or the London System | Caro-Kann Defence | Queen's Gambit Declined or the Slav |
| Flexible | 1.Nf3 or the English Opening | French Defence | Nimzo-Indian Defence |

## A repertoire by level

**Beginners (up to about 1000):** play open positions where tactics and principles matter most. 1.e4 e5 positions such as the Italian Game teach development, centre control and king safety better than anything else.

**Improving players (about 1000–1600):** pick **one** reply to 1.e4 and **one** to 1.d4, and stay with them. Learn the main ideas and the typical middlegame plans, about 6–10 moves deep. Switching openings every month is the most common reason players stop improving.

**Tournament players (1600+):** deepen your lines, prepare against the openings your regular opponents play, and keep a personal opening file with the lines you have analysed.

## How to study an opening the right way

1. **Learn the ideas first.** For each opening, write down in one sentence the main plan for each side, and the typical pawn breaks.
2. **Play through 10–20 model games** by strong players in your opening. You will absorb the typical plans, piece placements and endgames.
3. **Learn the critical tricks and traps** in your lines, for both sides, so you don't lose in 15 moves.
4. **Play it in practice games**, then check your games afterwards. Where did you leave the book? What should you have played?
5. **Keep a repertoire file** (a Lichess study works well) and add to it after every game.

## Common opening mistakes

- **Memorising without understanding.** When your opponent plays a different move, the memorised line is useless. The ideas still help.
- **Grabbing pawns while undeveloped.** A pawn is rarely worth three tempi.
- **Copying a world champion's repertoire.** Their openings depend on deep preparation. Choose openings you can understand.
- **Ignoring the endgame.** Many openings lead to typical endgames. Knowing them makes your opening choice much stronger.
