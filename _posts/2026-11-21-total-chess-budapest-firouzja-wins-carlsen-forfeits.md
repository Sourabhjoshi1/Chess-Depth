The first event of chess's newest championship has its winner. **Alireza Firouzja** won the Total Chess World Championship Tour Pilot in Budapest on Friday, coming back from a set down against **Anish Giri** and holding a draw in the Armageddon decider. But the event will also be remembered for something that happened far from the title race: **Magnus Carlsen** choosing not to turn up for games he no longer needed.

Here is what happened, how the new format works, and what our students can take from it.

## What is Total Chess?

Total Chess is a new championship created by Norway Chess and approved by FIDE. The idea is simple: find the most *complete* player by mixing three time controls in one event, **blitz, rapid and "fast classic"** (45 minutes plus 30 seconds a move). Former World Champion Ding Liren compared it to a triathlon.

Budapest (10 to 20 November) was a **pilot**, a test run with 24 of the world's best players. The full tour starts in 2027, when the overall winner will be crowned **FIDE World Combined Champion**, and the tour will also send two players to the 2028 Candidates.

## How the format works

- **Opening blitz event:** a nine-round blitz Swiss to set the seedings. Carlsen won it unbeaten with 7/9.
- **Group stage:** four groups of six, playing a double round-robin of fast classic games.
- **Knockouts:** everyone played on, in six brackets deciding every place from 1st to 24th. Each match lasted two days, and each day had **4 blitz games (1 point a win), 2 rapid games (4 points a win) and 1 fast classic game (8 points a win)**.

What matters is who wins each *day*, not by how much. If the players win one day each, an **Armageddon game** decides the match, and the player with more total points picks the colour.

That weighting is important for the rest of this story: one fast classic game is worth as much as everything else that day put together.

## The final: Firouzja comes back

Firouzja, Giri, Fabiano Caruana and Maxime Vachier-Lagrave won the four groups. Giri beat Vachier-Lagrave on both days without losing a game. Firouzja needed an Armageddon to get past Caruana, after Caruana won the fast classic game on day two and Firouzja then won the decider when Caruana ran out of time.

In the final, Giri won day one **11–9**, thanks mainly to the blitz. Firouzja hit back to win day two **12–8**. That set up an Armageddon, and because Firouzja had more points over the two days (21 to 19), he chose **Black with draw odds**. He held a draw in 35 moves and took the title and **$80,000**.

## How the Indian players did

- **Arjun Erigaisi** reached the final of his bracket but lost both days to Haik Martirosyan, finishing **10th**. On day two he led 9–3 going into the fast classic game, and that single game decided the day.
- **R. Praggnanandhaa** won his last match comfortably and finished **21st**.
- **R. Vaishali** had the result of the opening blitz, beating Hans Niemann and Wesley So, and later beat Arjun in the group stage. She finished **24th** after a very hard final day against Nodirbek Abdusattorov.
- World Champion **Gukesh** did not play: his title match against Javokhir Sindarov starts in Geneva on 25 November.

## The Carlsen forfeits

Carlsen had a difficult event. Wins in fast classic would not come, Martirosyan beat him in the group stage, and he finished fifth in his group. That put him in the bracket for **17th place**.

Then, three times in four days, he did not appear for a fast classic game:

1. **Against Bibisara Assaubayeva, day one.** Carlsen won the blitz and both rapid games (11.5 out of 12), so the day was already his. He did not come for the fast classic game. Assaubayeva waited at the board until she was given the win by forfeit.
2. **Against Assaubayeva, day two.** The same happened again. The day finished 10–10, which was enough for Carlsen to go through.
3. **Against Daniil Artemiev, in the match for 17th.** On day one Carlsen played and won the fast classic game, his first win of the event at that time control. On day two he did not appear, Artemiev won the day, and Carlsen then won the Armageddon to finish 17th.

### Why would he do that?

Two reasons were widely discussed.

**Rating.** FIDE does not normally rate games played after a match is decided, but the organisers had asked for those games to be rated, and players were told at the technical meeting (which Carlsen missed) that every game would count. A forfeit, on the other hand, is not rated. Reports said a draw against Assaubayeva would have taken Carlsen below **2800** for the first time in about 17 years, and a loss could even have put his **world No. 1** ranking at risk.

**Carlsen's own view.** He told Norwegian TV that the match was over and that not showing up only meant losing a game that did not matter. He also said he would rather not play much more classical chess, and that the format has a lot of potential but was too complicated the first time.

### The reaction

Opinions were sharply split:

- **Critics** said it disrespected the opponent and the organisers. Hans Niemann called it favouritism, arguing that any other player would have lost the whole match. Ian Nepomniachtchi said a player who does this should be removed from the tournament.
- **Others** defended him. Some players said they would have done the same with a ranking at stake. Caruana compared it to a tennis player giving up a set and coming back for the next one.
- **The organisers** said the rules are the same for everyone and nothing had been broken: a player who does not appear simply loses that game. Viswanathan Anand said there is a gap in the rules that needs tightening, and Total Chess has said the pilot is a learning exercise, with the format under review before the tour starts in March 2027.

## What students can learn from Budapest

1. **Play every game properly.** At the top level a forfeit is a business decision. For a student, every game is practice you can't get back, and your opponent came to play you.
2. **Know the scoring before you play.** In this format one fast classic game was worth as much as seven others. In any event, understanding how points are counted changes how you should manage risk.
3. **A bad start can be recovered.** Firouzja lost the first day of the final and still won the title. Arjun led 9–3 and still lost the day. Nothing is decided until the last game ends.
4. **Be complete.** The whole idea of Total Chess is that the best player should be strong at every speed. Practise blitz for intuition, rapid for decisions, and longer games for real calculation.

Congratulations to Alireza Firouzja, the first Total Chess champion. We'll be following the tour, and the Gukesh–Sindarov World Championship match in Geneva, closely.
