CCT in Chess: Checks, Captures and Threats

What CCT means in chess, how to use the checks, captures and threats routine on every move, three solved examples, common mistakes, and a free CCT workbook.

ChessDepth Coaching Team · 5 October 2026 · 6 min read · All levels

CCT in chess stands for checks, captures and threats. Before you choose a move, you look at every check, then every capture, then every threat, for you and for your opponent. It is the simplest way to find tactics and to stop blundering pieces.

Most games below master level are not lost to brilliant combinations. They are lost to simple oversights: a piece left hanging, a check that was not noticed, a threat that was ignored. The CCT method is the cure. It is a short checklist you run before every important move:

Checks · Captures · Threats

It sounds almost too simple. But strong players do this automatically, and the more you practise it, the faster and more natural it becomes.

Why CCT works

Forcing moves limit your opponent’s choices. A check must be answered. A capture usually must be recaptured. A threat must be dealt with. Because forcing moves narrow the possibilities, they are the moves you can calculate accurately, and they are where tactics hide.

Looking at forcing moves first also protects you: if you check your opponent’s forcing moves too, you will notice most of their tricks before they happen.

Step 1: Checks

List every check you can give, even ones that look silly. Many winning combinations start with a check that loses material at first sight. Ask:

  • After this check, where can the king go?
  • Can the check be blocked or the checking piece captured?
  • Is it checkmate?
Show the answer

1.Rd8#. The black king is trapped behind its own pawns on f7, g7 and h7, and nothing can block or capture on d8. The black queen on b2 looks dangerous, but checks come first, and this one ends the game.

This is the classic back-rank mate. If you had only looked at the black queen’s threats, you might have played a defensive move and missed an instant win.

Step 2: Captures

Next, look at every capture, for both sides:

  • Which of my pieces can capture something?
  • Which enemy pieces are undefended (“loose pieces drop off”)?
  • If I capture, what does my opponent recapture with, and who comes out ahead?

Count attackers and defenders. If you attack a piece twice and it is defended once, you can usually win it.

Some captures are strong because of what the captured piece was doing. Look for the defender: if one piece guards a key square, capturing it can open the door.

Show the answer

1.Bxf6! The knight on f6 was the only piece guarding h7. Now 1…Qxf6 or 1…gxf6 runs into 2.Qxh7#, because the bishop on d3 supports the queen. And if Black stops the mate with 1…g6 or 1…h6, the bishop on f6 takes the queen on d8. Black cannot deal with both.

Step 3: Threats

Finally, look for moves that create a threat your opponent must answer:

  • Attacking an undefended or more valuable piece.
  • Threatening checkmate.
  • Setting up a fork, a pin or a skewer next move.
  • Attacking a pinned piece.

Threats are slower than checks and captures, so check whether your opponent has a forcing reply first. The best threats are double threats: two things at once, so your opponent can only stop one.

Show the answer

1.Qd3! It is a quiet move, but it makes two threats: 2.Qxh7#, with the bishop on b1 backing up the queen, and 2.Qxa6, taking the undefended knight. Black can stop the mate with 1…g6 or 1…f5, but then 2.Qxa6 wins a piece. (If 1…Qh4 guards h7, 2.Qxd7 takes the bishop instead.)

Don’t forget your opponent’s CCT

The second half of the method is defensive. Before you play your move, ask:

“After my move, what are my opponent’s checks, captures and threats?”

This one question prevents most blunders. If your move leaves a piece loose, walks into a fork or allows a back-rank check, you will usually see it now, not after your opponent plays it.

Common CCT mistakes

  • Stopping at the first good move. You find a capture that wins a pawn and play it, missing the check that wins the queen. Finish the list.
  • Only checking your own moves. Half of CCT is defence. Most blunders happen because nobody asked what the opponent’s checks and captures were.
  • Dismissing “silly” checks. A queen sacrifice with check is still a check. List it, then reject it if it fails.
  • Forgetting the recapture. A capture is only good if the trade that follows is good. Count to the end of the exchange.
  • Doing it only in puzzles. In a puzzle you know there is a tactic. In a game you must look on every move, because nobody tells you when it is there.

Putting CCT into your thinking process

On every move in a serious game:

  1. What did my opponent’s last move change? What does it threaten?
  2. Run CCT for me: checks, captures, threats.
  3. If nothing forcing works, make a positional move (improve your worst piece, follow your plan).
  4. Blunder check: run CCT for your opponent after your chosen move.
  5. Play the move.

It takes a few seconds once it becomes a habit, and it saves whole games.

How to practise

  • Solve puzzles with the checklist. Before choosing a move, say the list out loud: “Checks: … Captures: … Threats: …”
  • Play longer games (15 minutes or more per side) so you have time to build the habit. In blitz, the habit gets skipped.
  • Review your losses and mark every moment you missed a check, a capture or a threat. You will see the same patterns repeating.
  • Train your visualization. CCT only works if you see the board clearly in your head two or three moves ahead. Our free Blindfold Chess Training builds exactly that, starting with simple drills (here is how to play blindfold chess).

Frequently asked questions

What does CCT stand for in chess?

CCT stands for checks, captures and threats. It is a thinking routine: before choosing a move, you look at every check, then every capture, then every threat, first for yourself and then for your opponent.

Why do you look at checks first?

Checks are the most forcing moves: the opponent must answer them, so there are only a few replies to calculate. Many combinations, including back-rank mates, start with a check that looks risky at first sight.

Do I have to use CCT on every move?

Use the full routine whenever the position has tension: pieces attacking each other, an exposed king, or after your opponent’s move changes something. In quiet positions, a quick check of your opponent’s checks, captures and threats is enough to avoid most blunders.

Does CCT work in blitz?

Yes, once it is a habit. Build it in slower games (15 minutes or more per side) and with puzzles first. Strong players run CCT in a second or two because they have practised it thousands of times.

How can I practise CCT?

Solve puzzles while saying the list out loud, play longer games, and check your losses for missed checks, captures and threats. Our free Checks, Captures and Threats Workbook has 20 graded problems with full answers.
